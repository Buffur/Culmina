   6  # Plan an interior palette
   7  
   8  ## Read the room
   9  
  10  - Identify room type, location or hemisphere when relevant, window orientation, daylight level, artificial lighting, size, fixed materials, furniture to retain, and desired mood.
  11  - Treat photographs as lighting-dependent evidence, not calibrated color measurements.
  12  - Ask about a missing fixed finish or lighting condition only when it would substantially alter the recommendation; otherwise state assumptions.
  13  
  14  ## Build the scheme
  15  
  16  1. Choose a dominant field color, supporting color, trim or ceiling direction, grounding neutral, and one or two accents.
  17  2. Coordinate undertones with immovable materials such as flooring, stone, tile, countertops, and timber. Avoid mixing conflicting warm and cool neutrals accidentally.
  18  3. Account for light: low or cool daylight can flatten chilly colors; strong warm light can intensify warm hues. Present this as guidance, not a guarantee.
  19  4. Consider proportion and placement. A successful accent object color may be oppressive across four walls.
  20  5. Keep adjacent rooms and sightlines coherent when the user describes them.
  21  6. Distinguish digital hex approximations from purchasable paint specifications. Recommend physical samples viewed at different times of day before purchase; never fabricate manufacturer color matches.
  22  
  23  ## Deliver
  24  
  25  - Map each color to a surface or material and give a short reason for the complete scheme.
  26  - Invoke `palette-picker` with ordered uppercase `#RRGGBB` approximations, from dominant architectural colors through accents, and explain the room assumptions in `reason`.
